=== lesson
## 1. Origins and the early Republic
Rome sits on the **Tiber River** in central Italy, with good farmland and a central position in the Mediterranean. Legend says **Romulus and Remus** founded it in **753 BCE**. Early Rome was ruled by kings and strongly influenced by its northern neighbors, the **Etruscans** (engineering, the arch, the toga, gladiator contests, and the alphabet they had borrowed from the Greeks). Greek colonies in the south passed on Greek gods, art, and ideas.

>> Romans hate the arrogant Etruscan king **Tarquin the Proud** | They expel him in **509 BCE** | They create a **republic** ("res publica," the public thing) designed so that no one man could ever be king again

### Government of the Republic
| Institution | Who / how | Power |
|---|---|---|
| **Consuls** (2) | Elected for **1 year** | Ran government and commanded armies; each could **veto** the other |
| **Senate** | About 300 men, originally **patricians**, served for life | Controlled finances and foreign policy; advised magistrates; most prestige |
| **Dictator** | Appointed in emergency for **max 6 months** | Absolute power. Model: **Cincinnatus**, who saved Rome, then returned to his plow |
| **Tribunes** | Elected by the plebeians | Protected plebeians; could **veto** ("I forbid") acts of magistrates and the Senate |
| **Assemblies** | Citizens voting in groups | Elected magistrates and passed laws |
| Praetors, censors | Elected officials | Justice; census and public morals |

The Greek historian **Polybius** admired this **mixed constitution**: consuls (monarchy), Senate (aristocracy), and assemblies (democracy) checking one another. Its ideas of **checks and balances** and **veto** influenced later republics, including the United States.

### Struggle of the Orders (494–287 BCE)
Rome's society split into **patricians** (wealthy landowning aristocrats) and **plebeians** (the common majority: farmers, artisans, merchants). Plebeians fought for equality mostly by **refusing to fight** (going on strike, called "secession"), since the army needed them.

- **494 BCE:** plebeians win their own officials, the **tribunes**.
- **c. 450 BCE:** the **Twelve Tables**, Rome's first written laws, posted publicly in the Forum so patrician judges could not twist unwritten custom.
- **367 BCE:** one consul could be (later had to be) a plebeian.
- **287 BCE:** the **Hortensian Law** made decisions of the plebeian assembly binding on all Romans.

In practice, rich plebeians merged with patricians into a new ruling class (the *nobiles*), so Rome never became a true democracy.

**Roman values:** the father of the family (*paterfamilias*) held legal power over his household; society ran on **patron-client** relationships (powerful patrons protected clients, who gave loyalty and votes); and Romans prized ***pietas*** (duty to gods, family, and state), ***gravitas*** (seriousness), and the "**custom of the ancestors**" (*mos maiorum*).

## 2. Conquest of the Mediterranean
Rome conquered Italy by about 265 BCE and treated many defeated peoples generously, making them **allies** or granting partial **citizenship**. Loyal allies supplied soldiers, and **roads** linked them to Rome.

Then came the **Punic Wars** against **Carthage**, the Phoenician-founded trading power of North Africa:

| War | Dates | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | 264–241 BCE | Fought over **Sicily**; Rome built its **first navy** and won Sicily, its first province |
| **Second** | 218–201 BCE | **Hannibal** crossed the **Alps with elephants**, crushed Rome at **Cannae (216 BCE)**, but couldn't take the city; **Scipio Africanus** invaded Africa and beat him at **Zama (202 BCE)** |
| **Third** | 149–146 BCE | "**Carthage must be destroyed**" (**Cato the Elder**); Rome razed Carthage and enslaved its people |

Also in **146 BCE**, Rome destroyed Corinth; Macedonia and Greece became Roman. By the first century BCE, the Mediterranean was a Roman lake: *mare nostrum*, "our sea."

## 3. Why conquest wrecked the Republic
>> Conquest floods Italy with **slaves**, plunder, and cheap grain; rich men build huge slave-run estates, the **latifundia** | Small farmers, away at war for years, lose their land and drift into Rome as a jobless urban poor | Huge wealth gap, angry mobs, and ambitious generals: the Republic slides into civil war

- **The Gracchi brothers:** **Tiberius Gracchus** (tribune, 133 BCE) proposed giving public land to the poor; senators killed him. His brother **Gaius Gracchus** (123–121 BCE) pushed land reform and **cheap grain** for the urban poor and was also killed. Political violence was now normal. Politicians split into **optimates** (Senate party) and **populares** (who appealed to the people).
- **Gaius Marius** (consul 107 BCE and many times after) reformed the army by recruiting **landless volunteers** who were paid and promised land. Soldiers now looked to their **general**, not the state, for rewards: **private armies**.
- **Sulla** used his army to **march on Rome** (88 BCE), then made himself dictator (82 BCE) and killed his enemies.
- **Spartacus**, a gladiator, led a massive **slave revolt (73–71 BCE)**, crushed by Crassus.

### Julius Caesar and the end of the Republic
- **First Triumvirate (60 BCE):** an informal alliance of **Julius Caesar**, **Pompey**, and **Crassus**.
- Caesar conquered **Gaul** (58–50 BCE) and won a loyal army and fame.
- Ordered to disband, he **crossed the Rubicon** River with his army (**49 BCE**), starting civil war and defeating Pompey.
- As dictator he reformed debt and land, expanded citizenship, enlarged the Senate, and created the 365-day **Julian calendar**. In early 44 BCE he was named **dictator for life**.
- On the **Ides of March, 44 BCE**, senators led by **Brutus** and **Cassius** stabbed him to death to "save the Republic." The orator **Cicero**, the Republic's great defender, was murdered the next year.
- **Second Triumvirate (43 BCE):** **Octavian** (Caesar's adopted heir), **Mark Antony**, and Lepidus. Octavian then defeated Antony and **Cleopatra** of Egypt at **Actium (31 BCE)**.

## 4. The Empire and the Pax Romana
In **27 BCE**, the Senate gave Octavian the title **Augustus** ("revered one"). He called himself ***princeps*** ("first citizen") and **kept the Republic's outward forms** (Senate, consuls, elections) while actually controlling the army, provinces, and treasury. This system is the **Principate**.

Augustus created a **professional standing army** and the **Praetorian Guard**, built a salaried civil service, took censuses, and rebuilt Rome ("found a city of brick and left it marble"). His reign began the **Pax Romana** ("Roman Peace," **27 BCE–180 CE**): two centuries of relative peace, trade, and prosperity across the Mediterranean.

Emperors to know:

- **Julio-Claudians:** Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius (conquered Britain), **Nero** (blamed Christians for the **Great Fire of 64 CE**).
- **Flavians:** Vespasian (began the **Colosseum**); his son **Titus** destroyed **Jerusalem's Temple in 70 CE** during the Jewish revolt.
- **"Five Good Emperors"** (96–180 CE), each choosing a capable adopted heir: Nerva, **Trajan** (empire's **greatest extent**, 117 CE), **Hadrian** (stopped expanding; **Hadrian's Wall** in Britain), Antoninus Pius, and **Marcus Aurelius** (a Stoic philosopher who wrote the ***Meditations***). His unfit son **Commodus** ended the good run.

Cities had public baths, forums, and amphitheaters. Emperors kept the urban poor quiet with free grain and games: "**bread and circuses**." In **212 CE**, Emperor Caracalla granted **citizenship to all free men** in the empire.

## 5. Roman law, thought, and culture
**Law** may be Rome's greatest legacy. It grew from the Twelve Tables to the ***ius civile*** (law for citizens) and the ***ius gentium*** ("**law of nations**," applied to all peoples), and jurists linked it to **natural law**, the Stoic idea of universal principles of justice knowable by reason. Principles such as **innocent until proven guilty** and judging intent survived through **Justinian's Code** (Unit 4) into modern European law.

Romans admired and absorbed Greek culture (**Greco-Roman** civilization) but were more **practical**:

- **Engineering:** the **arch**, **concrete**, **aqueducts**, a vast **road** network, the **Colosseum**, and the domed **Pantheon**.
- **Literature (the "Golden Age" under Augustus):** **Virgil**'s ***Aeneid***, an epic linking Rome's founding to the Trojan hero Aeneas and glorifying Augustus; **Horace** (odes); **Ovid** (*Metamorphoses*; exiled by Augustus); **Livy**'s history of Rome from its founding, full of moral lessons.
- **History & thought:** **Tacitus** criticized imperial tyranny and, in *Germania*, praised Germanic tribes' simple virtues to shame Roman decadence. **Cicero** = Latin prose and oratory, defense of the Republic and natural law. **Lucretius** spread Epicurean philosophy; **Seneca** and **Marcus Aurelius** were **Stoics**. **Stoicism** fit Roman ideals of duty.
- **Science:** **Galen** (medicine, dominant for 1,400 years) and **Ptolemy** (a geocentric, Earth-centered model of the universe, accepted until Copernicus). Both wrote in Greek.
- **Latin** became the root of the **Romance languages** (Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian).

## 6. Early Christianity
**Jesus of Nazareth** (c. 4 BCE–c. 30 CE) was a Jewish teacher in **Judea**, then under Roman rule. He preached love of God and neighbor, forgiveness, and the coming **Kingdom of God**. The Roman governor **Pontius Pilate** had him **crucified**; his followers proclaimed his **resurrection** and called him the **Christ** (Messiah).

**Paul of Tarsus**, a Jewish convert, was the key missionary. He took the faith to **Gentiles** (non-Jews), taught that salvation came through **faith in Christ** rather than following Jewish law, and wrote letters (**Epistles**) that form much of the New Testament. **Peter**, leader of the apostles, was martyred in Rome; the **bishop of Rome (the pope)** later claimed authority as Peter's successor (the **Petrine doctrine**).

>> Pax Romana's safe **roads and sea lanes**, a common Greek language, and a message of **salvation and equality** open to all, including slaves, women, and the poor | Christianity spreads city to city through tight-knit communities that care for widows and the sick | Despite persecution, Christians become a large minority by 300 CE

Romans tolerated most religions, but Christians **refused to sacrifice to the emperor and Roman gods**, which seemed disloyal. Persecution came in waves: **Nero (64 CE)**, **Decius (250)**, and the **Great Persecution** under **Diocletian (303)**. **Martyrs** inspired others.

The Church organized itself on Roman lines: **bishops** headed city churches (**dioceses**), and bishops of great cities (Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, later Constantinople) gained special authority.

**Church Fathers** shaped Christian thought:

- **Jerome:** translated the Bible into Latin, the **Vulgate**.
- **Ambrose:** bishop of Milan; insisted the emperor was **within the Church, not above it** (forced Theodosius to do penance).
- **Augustine of Hippo** (354–430): ***Confessions*** (his conversion) and ***City of God***, written after the **410 sack of Rome**, contrasting the doomed **earthly city** with the eternal **City of God**. He stressed **original sin** and God's **grace**.

## 7. Crisis and reform: the late Empire
**Crisis of the Third Century (235–284 CE):** some 20+ emperors in 50 years, most murdered; armies making and unmaking emperors; invasions on the Rhine, Danube, and from Persia; **plague**; and runaway **inflation** as emperors **debased the coinage** to pay soldiers.

**Diocletian** (284–305) restored order with force:

- Split the empire into **East and West** and created the **Tetrarchy** (rule of four: two senior emperors and two juniors).
- Issued a **price edict** (wage and price controls, which failed), raised taxes, and tied people to their jobs: tenant farmers (**coloni**) were bound to the land, an early step toward **serfdom**.
- Launched the **Great Persecution** of Christians (303).

**Constantine** (306–337) reversed course on religion:

- Before the **Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312)** he reportedly saw a vision of the cross ("**In this sign, conquer**"), and won.
- The **Edict of Milan (313)** granted **toleration** to Christianity.
- He called the **Council of Nicaea (325)**, which condemned **Arianism** (the belief that Jesus was a created being, lesser than God the Father) and produced the **Nicene Creed**.
- He moved the capital east to **Constantinople** (founded **330** on old Byzantium), a sign that the empire's wealth lay in the East.

**Theodosius I** made Christianity the **official state religion (380)** and banned pagan sacrifices. After his death in **395**, the empire **split permanently** into Western and Eastern (Byzantine) halves.

## 8. The Germanic invasions and the fall of the West
>> **Huns** from Central Asia push west (370s) | Germanic peoples flee into the empire: the **Visigoths** cross the Danube (376) and destroy a Roman army at **Adrianople (378)**, killing Emperor Valens | The West can no longer control its borders; Germanic kingdoms replace Roman rule by 476

- **410:** **Alaric** and the **Visigoths** **sack Rome**, the first time in 800 years.
- **451:** **Attila the Hun** is checked at the **Catalaunian Plains** (Châlons) in Gaul; in 452 **Pope Leo I** meets Attila and persuades him to spare Rome.
- **455:** the **Vandals** (from their kingdom in **North Africa**) sack Rome.
- **476:** the Germanic general **Odoacer** deposes the last western emperor, the boy **Romulus Augustulus**: the traditional "**fall of Rome**." The **Eastern Empire** continued for nearly 1,000 more years.

**Why did the West fall?** Historians list many interacting causes: **political** (instability, civil wars), **economic** (heavy taxes, inflation, declining trade and cities), **military** (overstretched borders, reliance on Germanic mercenaries and *foederati*, "federated" allies, who had their own loyalties), **division** (the richer East looked after itself), and **external pressure** (the Huns and migrating Germanic peoples). Edward Gibbon (18th century) also blamed Christianity and lost civic virtue, a view that is debated.

**New Germanic kingdoms (c. 500):** **Ostrogoths** in Italy (King **Theodoric**), **Visigoths** in Spain, **Franks** in Gaul, **Anglo-Saxons** in Britain, **Vandals** in North Africa.

**Germanic customs:** loyalty of warriors to their chief (the ***comitatus***, a root of feudal loyalty); **unwritten customary law**; ***wergild*** ("man-money," a fine paid to the victim's family, scaled by the victim's rank, instead of blood feud); **trial by ordeal**. Most Goths were **Arian** Christians, which separated them from their Catholic Roman subjects.

## Big picture for the exam
Know the **Republic's institutions** and the **plebeian gains**; the **Punic Wars**; the chain **conquest → latifundia → Gracchi → generals' private armies → Caesar → Augustus**; how Augustus **disguised monarchy** as Republic; Roman **law, engineering, and writers**; **Paul** and **Augustine**; **Diocletian vs. Constantine**; and the **sequence of invasions** ending in **476**.

=== mustknow
- **509 BCE** Republic: 2 **consuls** (1 year, mutual veto), **Senate** (finance, foreign policy), **tribunes** (plebeian veto), emergency **dictator** (6 months; Cincinnatus).
- **Struggle of the Orders:** tribunes (494), **Twelve Tables** (c. 450, first written law), plebeian consul (367), **Hortensian Law** (287).
