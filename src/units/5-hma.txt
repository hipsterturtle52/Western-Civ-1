intro: From about 1000 to 1450, Europe revived: a reformed and powerful papacy, the Crusades, booming towns, stronger monarchies, universities, and Gothic cathedrals. Then the 14th century brought famine, plague, war, and a divided Church. Second half of Medieval History (23–27%).

=== lesson
## 1. The medieval revival (c. 1000–1300)
After the Viking and Magyar raids ended, Europe grew. Warmer weather, the **heavy plow**, the **horse collar**, and the **three-field system** produced more food, so **population roughly doubled** between 1000 and 1300. More people meant more land cleared, more trade, and the growth of towns.

>> Agricultural improvements and the end of invasions | Population growth, surplus to sell, revived long-distance trade | **Towns** and a **money economy** grow, and with them a new middle class that doesn't fit the feudal order

## 2. Towns, trade, and guilds
- **Trade routes:** Italian cities (**Venice**, **Genoa**, Pisa) dominated Mediterranean trade with Byzantium and the Islamic world (spices, silk). In the north, the **Hanseatic League** (Lübeck, Hamburg, and other German towns) controlled Baltic and North Sea trade (timber, fish, furs, grain). The two zones met at the great **fairs of Champagne** in France.
- **Towns** bought or won **charters** from lords and kings granting self-government and freedom from feudal duties. A serf who lived in a town for a year and a day became free: "**town air makes you free**." Townspeople were called **burghers** or the **bourgeoisie**.
- **Guilds** organized economic life. **Merchant guilds** controlled a town's trade; **craft guilds** (bakers, weavers, smiths) set prices, wages, and quality standards and limited competition. Training went **apprentice → journeyman → master** (who had to produce a "masterpiece").
- **Money and banking** returned: bills of exchange and credit, pioneered by Italian merchants. The Church's ban on **usury** (charging interest) pushed many Jews, excluded from guilds and landholding, into moneylending, which fed resentment and persecution.

## 3. The reformed Church at its height
Around 1000, the Church had problems: nobles controlled church offices, some priests married, and offices were bought and sold (**simony**).

- **Cluny** (founded **910** in France): a Benedictine monastery placed directly under the pope, free from local lords. Its reform movement spread to hundreds of houses.
- **Pope Gregory VII** (1073–1085) banned simony and clerical marriage and insisted that **only the Church could appoint bishops**.

>> Who appoints bishops, the king or the pope? (bishops were also great landholders and royal officials) | **Investiture Controversy**: Gregory VII excommunicates Emperor **Henry IV**, who stands barefoot in the snow at **Canossa (1077)** to beg forgiveness | **Concordat of Worms (1122)**: the Church grants spiritual authority (ring and staff); the emperor may grant the lands. The papacy gains prestige, and German emperors lose ground

**Pope Innocent III** (1198–1216) marks the **peak of papal power**. He forced King **John** of England to submit by placing England under **interdict** (no church services or sacraments), launched the **Fourth Crusade** and a crusade against heretics in southern France, and held the **Fourth Lateran Council (1215)**, which defined **transubstantiation** (the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ) and required annual confession.

**Sacraments:** the seven sacraments (baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance, marriage, holy orders, last rites) were channels of grace that only the clergy could administer. **Excommunication** (cutting a person off from them) and **interdict** (shutting down church services in an entire region) were the Church's great weapons.

**New religious orders:**

- **Cistercians** (strict, rural monks; **Bernard of Clairvaux** preached the Second Crusade).
- **Mendicant ("begging") friars** worked **in towns** among the people instead of in cloisters: **Franciscans** (**Francis of Assisi**, radical poverty and service to the poor) and **Dominicans** (**Dominic**, educated preachers who fought heresy and later ran the **Inquisition**).

**Heresy:** the **Cathars (Albigensians)** of southern France believed in two gods (good/spiritual vs. evil/material) and rejected the Church. Innocent III's **Albigensian Crusade** (1209) crushed them. The **Waldensians** preached apostolic poverty.

## 4. The Crusades (1095–1291)
>> **Seljuk Turks** defeat the Byzantines at **Manzikert (1071)** and threaten Constantinople; Byzantine Emperor Alexius asks the West for help | **Pope Urban II** at the **Council of Clermont (1095)** calls a holy war to free Jerusalem, promising forgiveness of sins | Thousands of knights and commoners march east

Motives mixed **religious zeal** (pilgrimage, remission of sins), **papal ambition** (leading Christendom, reuniting with the East), **land and wealth** for younger sons (who inherited nothing under **primogeniture**), and **trade** for Italian cities.

| Crusade | Result |
|---|---|
| **First (1096–1099)** | The only clear success: **Jerusalem captured (1099)** with a massacre; four **crusader states** founded. Also massacres of Jews in the Rhineland on the way |
| **Second (1147–1149)** | Preached by Bernard of Clairvaux after Edessa fell; failed |
| **Third (1189–1192)** | After **Saladin** retook **Jerusalem (1187)**; the "Crusade of Kings": **Richard I** "the Lionheart" of England, **Philip II** of France, and **Frederick Barbarossa** (drowned on the way). Ended with a **truce** allowing Christian pilgrims access to Jerusalem |
| **Fourth (1202–1204)** | Diverted by Venice; crusaders **sacked Constantinople (1204)**, a fellow Christian city, deepening the East-West split and weakening Byzantium |

The last crusader stronghold, **Acre**, fell in **1291**. In Spain, the **Reconquista** (Christian reconquest of Muslim Iberia) continued until **1492**.

**Consequences:** more **trade** with the East (enriching **Venice and Genoa**), **contact with Islamic learning**, a **weaker Byzantium**, **anti-Jewish violence** in Europe, more power to monarchs (who taxed for crusades), and lasting Christian-Muslim bitterness.

## 5. Feudal monarchies: England, France, the Holy Roman Empire
### England: strong kings, limited by law
- **1066:** **William the Conqueror**, Duke of Normandy, wins the **Battle of Hastings**, takes England, and makes all landholders his vassals. The **Domesday Book (1086)** surveyed every estate for taxation.
- **Henry II** (1154–1189) expanded royal justice: traveling **circuit judges**, **juries**, and **common law** (law common to the whole kingdom, based on precedent). His clash with Archbishop **Thomas Becket** ended with Becket's murder in Canterbury Cathedral (1170).
- **King John** lost Normandy to France (1204), quarreled with Innocent III, and raised heavy taxes. His barons forced him to sign the **Magna Carta (1215)**: the king is **subject to the law**; no special taxes without the consent of the great council; no free man imprisoned or punished except by **lawful judgment of his peers** or the law of the land.
- **Parliament** grew from the king's council. **Edward I**'s **Model Parliament (1295)** included nobles, clergy, and representatives of towns and shires. Parliament later split into the **House of Lords** and **House of Commons** and gained **"power of the purse"** (approval of taxes).

### France: kings slowly gather power
- **Hugh Capet** (987) founded the **Capetian** dynasty, ruling only a small area around **Paris** but producing male heirs for centuries.
- **Philip II Augustus** (1180–1223) took **Normandy** from King John and governed through salaried royal officials (**bailiffs**).
- **Louis IX (Saint Louis)** (1226–1270) was famed for justice and piety and led two crusades.
- **Philip IV "the Fair"** (1285–1314) called the first **Estates-General (1302)** (clergy, nobles, townspeople) to back him against the pope, taxed the clergy, and expelled the Jews and destroyed the Knights Templar to seize their wealth.

### Holy Roman Empire: the failure to unify
German emperors (from **Otto I**, crowned 962) spent their energy fighting popes and trying to control **Italy**. **Frederick I Barbarossa** was defeated by the **Lombard League** of northern Italian cities at **Legnano (1176)**. The result: Germany and Italy stayed **fragmented** into hundreds of princely states and city-states. The **Golden Bull (1356)** made the emperor **elected by seven princes (electors)**, confirming weak central power.

## 6. Medieval thought and culture
**Universities** grew out of cathedral schools as guilds of teachers and students: **Bologna** (law), **Paris** (theology), **Oxford**, and Cambridge. Teaching was in Latin, based on lecture and debate. The core curriculum was the seven liberal arts.

**Scholasticism** was the method of using **reason and logic (especially Aristotle's) to explain and defend Christian faith**. Aristotle's works returned to the West via Arabic translations from Spain and Sicily.

- **Anselm of Canterbury:** "faith seeking understanding"; a logical proof of God's existence.
- **Peter Abelard:** ***Sic et Non*** ("Yes and No"), which set contradictory authorities side by side to train reasoning.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274), a Dominican: ***Summa Theologica***. He argued that **faith and reason do not conflict**, because both come from God, and harmonized **Aristotle** with Christian doctrine (natural law, the "five ways" of proving God).
- **William of Ockham:** "Ockham's razor" (prefer the simplest explanation); argued faith and reason were separate, undermining scholastic synthesis.
- **Roger Bacon** stressed observation and experiment.

**Architecture:**

| Romanesque (c. 1000–1150) | Gothic (from c. 1140) |
|---|---|
| **Rounded arches**, barrel vaults | **Pointed arches**, **ribbed vaults** |
| **Thick walls**, small windows, dark interiors | **Flying buttresses** carry the weight, allowing tall, thin walls |
| Heavy, fortress-like | Soaring height; huge **stained-glass** windows to fill the church with light |
| | First: **Abbey of Saint-Denis** (Abbot **Suger**, 1140s); Chartres, Notre-Dame de Paris |

**Literature** shifted from Latin toward **vernacular** (everyday) languages: troubadour songs of **courtly love** and the code of **chivalry**; epic *chansons de geste* such as the *Song of Roland*; **Dante**'s ***Divine Comedy*** (Italian, early 1300s: a journey through Hell, Purgatory, and Paradise); **Chaucer**'s ***Canterbury Tales*** (English, late 1300s: pilgrims of every class telling stories); and **Christine de Pizan**'s defense of women, *The Book of the City of Ladies* (1405).

## 7. The calamitous 14th century
- **Great Famine (1315–1317):** cold, rainy weather destroyed harvests; millions starved.
- **Black Death (1347–1351):** **bubonic plague**, carried by fleas on rats and spread along trade routes (arriving via Genoese ships from the Black Sea port of **Caffa**), killed about **one-third** of Europe's population. Reactions included **flagellants** whipping themselves in penance and **massacres of Jews**, who were falsely blamed for poisoning wells.

>> Black Death kills a third of the workforce | **Labor shortage**: surviving peasants and workers demand **higher wages** and freedom; lords try to freeze wages (England's Statute of Labourers, 1351) | **Peasant revolts** (French **Jacquerie** 1358; **English Peasants' Revolt 1381**, led by Wat Tyler, sparked by a poll tax), and **serfdom declines in Western Europe**

- **Hundred Years' War (1337–1453):** England's **Edward III** claimed the French throne. The English won early with the **longbow** at **Crécy (1346)**, **Poitiers (1356)**, and **Agincourt (1415, Henry V)**. Then **Joan of Arc**, a peasant girl inspired by visions, lifted the **siege of Orléans (1429)** and had Charles VII crowned; she was captured and **burned as a heretic (1431)**. France expelled the English (except Calais) by 1453.
- **Consequences:** a sense of **national identity** in both countries; the **decline of knights and chivalry** (longbows, then **gunpowder and cannon**, made armored cavalry obsolete); a **stronger French monarchy** (permanent taxes, a standing army); and in England, civil war: the **Wars of the Roses (1455–1485)**.

## 8. The late medieval Church in crisis
>> **Pope Boniface VIII** clashes with **Philip IV** of France over taxing clergy; his bull ***Unam Sanctam* (1302)** claims every human must submit to the pope | Philip's agents seize Boniface, who dies soon after; a French pope moves the papacy to **Avignon** (1309–1377): the "**Babylonian Captivity**" of the Church | Papal prestige falls; many see the popes as French puppets

- **Great Western Schism (1378–1417):** after the return to Rome, rival popes were elected in **Rome and Avignon** (later a third at Pisa), each excommunicating the other's followers.
- The **Council of Constance (1414–1418)** ended the schism by electing one pope. The **Conciliar Movement** argued that **church councils had authority over popes**, but popes soon reasserted control.
- Critics foreshadowed the Reformation: **John Wycliffe** (England, d. 1384) said the **Bible**, not the pope, was the supreme authority and inspired the first **English translation of the Bible**; his followers were the **Lollards**. **Jan Hus** (Bohemia), influenced by Wycliffe, was **burned at Constance (1415)** despite a promise of safe conduct; his followers, the **Hussites**, fought off crusades.

Meanwhile, in the east, the **Ottoman Turks** captured **Constantinople in 1453**, ending the Byzantine Empire.

Know the **Investiture Controversy** (Gregory VII, Henry IV, Canossa, Worms), **Innocent III**, the **friars**, the **Crusades' causes and results**, **towns and guilds**, **England's limits on kings** (Magna Carta → Parliament) vs. **France's centralizing kings** vs. **Germany's fragmentation**, **scholasticism and Aquinas**, **Romanesque vs. Gothic**, the **Black Death's effects**, the **Hundred Years' War**, and the **papacy's decline** (Avignon, Schism, Constance, Wycliffe, Hus).

Charter (town) :: Document from a lord or king granting a town self-government and freedoms.
Guilds :: Associations of merchants or craftsmen regulating prices, quality, and training (apprentice → journeyman → master).
Hanseatic League :: Alliance of north German trading towns dominating Baltic and North Sea commerce.
Simony :: The buying and selling of church offices.
Investiture Controversy :: Conflict over whether popes or kings appoint bishops; Canossa (1077); Concordat of Worms (1122).
Concordat of Worms :: 1122 compromise: the Church invests bishops with spiritual authority; the emperor grants their lands.
Innocent III :: Pope (1198–1216) at the height of papal power; interdict on England; Fourth Lateran Council.
Interdict :: Papal penalty suspending church services and sacraments in an entire region.
Mendicant orders :: Begging friars who worked in towns: Franciscans (poverty) and Dominicans (preaching, fighting heresy).
First Crusade :: Called by Urban II at Clermont (1095); captured Jerusalem (1099); founded crusader states.
Fourth Crusade :: Diverted by Venice; sacked Christian Constantinople (1204).
Saladin :: Muslim sultan who recaptured Jerusalem in 1187, prompting the Third Crusade.
Domesday Book :: William the Conqueror's 1086 survey of English landholding for taxation.
Common law :: English law based on royal court decisions and precedent, common to the whole kingdom (Henry II).
Magna Carta :: 1215 charter barons forced on King John: king subject to law; no taxes without council's consent; lawful judgment.

Scholasticism :: Medieval method using reason and Aristotle's logic to explain and defend Christian faith.
Summa Theologica :: Thomas Aquinas' synthesis of Aristotle and Christian theology; faith and reason agree.
