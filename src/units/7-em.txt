=== lesson
## 1. Opening the Atlantic: why and how
Exploration began in the 1400s and exploded in the 1500s. Motives are summed up as "**God, gold, and glory**":

- **Gold:** Europeans craved Asian **spices** and luxury goods, but the **Ottoman Turks** and **Venetian** middlemen controlled the eastern routes and took big profits. A **sea route to Asia** would cut them out.
- **God:** spreading **Christianity** (and the crusading spirit of the Reconquista).
- **Glory:** fame and power for explorers and monarchs; **New Monarchies** had the money and ambition to sponsor voyages.

**Technology** made it possible: the **caravel** (a small, nimble ship with triangular **lateen** sails that could sail against the wind), the **magnetic compass**, the **astrolabe** (for latitude), better maps, and shipboard **cannon**.

| Who | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Prince Henry the Navigator** (Portugal) | 1400s | Sponsored voyages down the West African coast |
| **Bartolomeu Dias** (Portugal) | **1488** | Rounded the **Cape of Good Hope** (southern tip of Africa) |
| **Christopher Columbus** (for Spain) | **1492** | Sailed west, reached the **Caribbean**, thinking it was Asia |
| **Treaty of Tordesillas** | **1494** | Spain and Portugal moved the pope's 1493 dividing line west: Spain got lands to the west, Portugal to the east (so Portugal got **Brazil**) |
| **Vasco da Gama** (Portugal) | **1498** | Reached **India** by sea around Africa |
| **Ferdinand Magellan** (for Spain) | **1519–1522** | First **circumnavigation**; Magellan was killed in the Philippines, and his crew finished the trip |

**Portugal** built a **trading-post empire** (forts at Goa in India and Malacca, controlling spice trade by force), not large colonies.

**Spain** built a land empire in the Americas through the **conquistadors**: **Hernán Cortés** conquered the **Aztecs** (capital Tenochtitlan, ruler Montezuma) in **1519–1521**, and **Francisco Pizarro** conquered the **Incas** (ruler Atahualpa) in **1532–1533**. Their advantages: **steel weapons, guns, horses**, **native allies** who hated Aztec rule, and above all **European diseases**.

>> Europeans arrive carrying **smallpox**, measles, and influenza, to which Native Americans had no immunity | Native populations collapse, by as much as **90%** in many areas | Spaniards force survivors to work (the **encomienda** labor system and silver mines like **Potosí**) and then import **enslaved Africans** to replace them

The Dominican friar **Bartolomé de Las Casas** denounced Spanish cruelty to Native Americans.

**Columbian Exchange:** the transfer of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the hemispheres. To Europe: **potatoes, maize (corn), tomatoes, tobacco, cacao**. To the Americas: **wheat, sugar, horses, cattle, pigs**, and **diseases**. Potatoes and maize later fueled European population growth.

**Atlantic slave trade:** **sugar plantations** in Brazil and the Caribbean demanded huge labor forces. Millions of Africans were shipped across the Atlantic in the brutal **Middle Passage**, part of a **triangular trade** (European goods to Africa → enslaved people to the Americas → sugar, tobacco, and other goods to Europe).

**Latecomers** in the 1600s: the **Dutch** (East Indies spice trade; New Amsterdam, 1620s), the **English** (Jamestown 1607; Plymouth 1620), and the **French** (**Quebec 1608**, fur trade).

## 2. The Commercial Revolution
>> Tons of **American silver** (especially from Potosí) pour into Spain and then all of Europe | Too much money chasing too few goods: the **Price Revolution**, a long **inflation** in which prices roughly quadrupled over the 1500s | Merchants and borrowers gain; landlords on fixed rents and wage-earners lose; **Spain** itself suffers because its silver flows out to pay for imports and wars

New business forms and ideas:

- **Joint-stock companies:** many investors pool money by buying **shares**, sharing profits and **spreading risk** for expensive, dangerous voyages. Examples: the **English East India Company (1600)** and the **Dutch East India Company (VOC, 1602)**, which had government-granted monopolies and their own armies.
- **Banking and finance:** the **Fugger** family of Augsburg lent to the Habsburgs (financing Charles V's election); **Amsterdam** became Europe's financial capital, with its **stock exchange** and the **Bank of Amsterdam (1609)**.
- **Putting-out (domestic) system:** merchants delivered raw materials such as wool to **rural households** to spin and weave at home, then collected and sold the finished cloth, **bypassing town guild restrictions**.
- **Mercantilism:** governments believed national wealth = **gold and silver (bullion)**. A nation should **export more than it imports** (favorable balance of trade) and use **colonies** as sources of raw materials and markets for finished goods, with tariffs and state regulation.
- **Capitalism:** private investment of capital for profit spread steadily.
- In England, **enclosure** (fencing common land for sheep) pushed peasants off the land. In **Eastern Europe**, by contrast, lords tightened **serfdom** to grow grain for export west (the "second serfdom").

## 3. Spain under Philip II and the Dutch Revolt
**Philip II** (1556–1598), son of Charles V, ruled Spain, the Americas, the Netherlands, and parts of Italy, and saw himself as the **champion of Catholicism**. He ruled from his palace-monastery, the **Escorial**.

- **Lepanto (1571):** a Holy League fleet led by Spain **defeated the Ottoman navy**, ending Ottoman naval dominance in the western Mediterranean.
- **Dutch Revolt (from 1566):** the wealthy, increasingly **Calvinist** Netherlands resented **Spanish taxes**, centralization, and the **Inquisition**. Philip sent the **Duke of Alba**, whose "Council of Troubles" ("**Council of Blood**") executed thousands. **William of Orange ("the Silent")** led the rebels. The seven northern provinces formed the **Union of Utrecht (1579)** and declared independence (1581) as the **United Provinces** (Dutch Republic). The southern provinces (modern Belgium) stayed Spanish and Catholic. Spain recognized Dutch independence only in **1648**.
- **Spanish Armada (1588):** Philip sent a huge fleet to invade Protestant **England** (Elizabeth I had aided the Dutch and executed the Catholic **Mary, Queen of Scots**, in 1587). Faster English ships, **fire ships**, and storms (the "Protestant wind") destroyed it.

>> Costly wars on many fronts + inflation + dependence on American silver | Spain declares **bankruptcy** repeatedly and loses the Armada | By the 1600s Spain declines, while the **Dutch Republic** and **England** rise as commercial and naval powers

**The Dutch Golden Age (1600s):** a Calvinist but relatively **tolerant** republic of merchants, the leading **shipping, banking, and trading** nation in Europe, and home of painters such as **Rembrandt** and **Vermeer**.

## 4. France: Wars of Religion to Richelieu
**French Wars of Religion (1562–1598):** Catholics (led by the **Guise** family) vs. **Huguenots** (French Calvinists, many of them nobles, led by the **Bourbons**). Religion mixed with **noble factions** competing for power under weak young kings and their mother, **Catherine de' Medici**.

- **St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre (August 24, 1572):** Catholic mobs killed thousands of Huguenots in Paris and the provinces after the wedding of the Protestant **Henry of Navarre**.
- Henry of Navarre became **Henry IV** (1589), the first **Bourbon** king. To win Catholic Paris, he **converted to Catholicism** (supposedly saying "**Paris is well worth a Mass**").
- **Edict of Nantes (1598):** granted **Huguenots freedom of worship** in many places, civil rights, and **fortified towns**. It reflected the **politiques**, who put **political unity and peace above religious uniformity**.

Henry IV was assassinated in 1610. Under **Louis XIII**, **Cardinal Richelieu** (chief minister 1624–1642) built royal absolutism:

- Used royal officials called **intendants** to govern the provinces and collect taxes, bypassing nobles.
- Destroyed Huguenot **political and military** power by besieging **La Rochelle** (1628) while letting them keep freedom of worship.
- Tore down nobles' castles and punished dueling and plots.
- Followed ***raison d'état*** ("**reason of state**"): national interest over religion. He supported **Protestant** Sweden and German princes against the **Catholic Habsburgs** in the Thirty Years' War.

## 5. England: Elizabeth to the Civil War
- **Elizabeth I** (1558–1603) set a moderate Protestant **Elizabethan Settlement** (1559; the **Thirty-Nine Articles**, 1563), avoided religious civil war, defeated the Armada, and presided over a cultural golden age (**Shakespeare**).
- **James I** (1603–1625), first **Stuart** king (already James VI of Scotland), believed in the **divine right of kings**: monarchs answer only to God. He sponsored the **King James Bible (1611)** and quarreled with Parliament over money.
- **Charles I** (1625–1649) raised taxes without Parliament's consent. In the **Petition of Right (1628)**, Parliament declared he could not levy **taxes without Parliament's consent**, **imprison people without cause**, **quarter soldiers** in private homes, or impose **martial law** in peacetime. He then ruled without Parliament (1629–1640), until a Scottish revolt forced him to recall it.
- The **English Civil War (1642–1649):** **Royalists ("Cavaliers")** vs. **Parliamentarians ("Roundheads")**, many of them **Puritans**. **Oliver Cromwell**'s **New Model Army** won, and Charles I was **executed in 1649**.

## 6. The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648)
**Causes:** the Peace of Augsburg (1555) had **excluded Calvinism**; Protestant and Catholic princes formed rival alliances; the **Habsburgs** wanted to strengthen imperial and Catholic power; and foreign powers (Denmark, Sweden, France) wanted to stop them.

| Phase | Dates | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Bohemian** | 1618–1625 | **Defenestration of Prague (1618)**: Bohemian Protestants throw imperial officials out a castle window and choose the Calvinist **Frederick V** as king; crushed at **White Mountain (1620)**; Bohemia forcibly re-Catholicized |
| **Danish** | 1625–1629 | Lutheran King Christian IV of Denmark intervenes and loses to imperial generals (**Wallenstein**); **Edict of Restitution (1629)** orders Protestants to return Church lands |
| **Swedish** | 1630–1635 | **Gustavus Adolphus** of Sweden, the "Lion of the North," wins at **Breitenfeld (1631)** but is killed at **Lützen (1632)** |
| **French (Swedish-French)** | 1635–1648 | **Catholic France** openly fights the Catholic Habsburgs on the Protestant side: **politics over religion** |

>> Armies of mercenaries live off the land for 30 years | Looting, famine, and disease kill perhaps **one-third** of the people in parts of **Germany** | Exhausted powers sign the **Peace of Westphalia (1648)**

**Peace of Westphalia (1648):**

- **Calvinism** added to Lutheranism and Catholicism as a legal option (princes still chose their state's religion).
- **Independence** of the **Dutch Republic** and the **Swiss Confederation** formally recognized.
- German princes became virtually **sovereign**, leaving the **Holy Roman Empire** a weak shell.
- **France** gained territory in **Alsace**; **Sweden** gained Baltic coastal lands.
- Often called the beginning of the modern **state system**: **sovereign states** that are equal under law, no outside interference in internal religion, and a **balance of power**. It marked the **end of the age of religious wars**, and the pope's protests were ignored.

## 7. Thought and culture
### The Scientific Revolution begins
>> Renaissance recovery of ancient texts + new instruments + challenges to authority | Scientists test old authorities (**Aristotle, Ptolemy, Galen**) against **observation, mathematics, and experiment** | A new view of the universe, and conflict with Church teaching

- **Nicolaus Copernicus**, *On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres* (**1543**): a **heliocentric** (Sun-centered) universe, replacing Ptolemy's Earth-centered model.
- **Andreas Vesalius**, *On the Fabric of the Human Body* (also **1543**): accurate **anatomy** from dissection, correcting Galen.
- **Tycho Brahe:** extremely precise naked-eye observations of the planets.
- **Johannes Kepler** (using Tycho's data): planets move in **elliptical orbits**, not perfect circles.
- **Galileo Galilei:** used the new **telescope** to study the heavens (1609–1610), and his published discoveries made it famous: **moons of Jupiter**, mountains on the Moon, phases of Venus; laws of **motion** and falling bodies. His *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632) defended Copernicus; the **Inquisition** tried him in **1633** and forced him to recant.
- **William Harvey** (1628): the **circulation of the blood**, pumped by the heart.
- **Francis Bacon**: **empiricism** and **inductive** reasoning, building general conclusions from many careful observations and experiments (the scientific method).
- **René Descartes**: **deductive** reasoning from first principles and mathematics; universal doubt led to "**I think, therefore I am**" (*Discourse on Method*, 1637); **mind–body dualism**; analytic geometry.
- (Isaac **Newton**'s laws of motion and gravity, 1687, completed this revolution after our period.)

### Political thought
- **Jean Bodin** (France, 1576): the state must have absolute, undivided **sovereignty** to keep order; a leading **politique**.
- **Divine right of kings:** **James I** argued that kings were God's lieutenants on earth.
- **Hugo Grotius** (Dutch), *On the Law of War and Peace* (1625): founder of **international law**, based on natural law.
- **Michel de Montaigne**, *Essays* (1580): **skepticism** ("What do I know?") and tolerance, a reaction to religious fanaticism.

### Literature and art
- **William Shakespeare** (1564–1616): plays exploring human nature (*Hamlet*, *King Lear*, *Macbeth*). **Miguel de Cervantes**, ***Don Quixote*** (1605–1615), satirized outdated **chivalric** ideals.
- **Mannerism** (after the High Renaissance): elongated, distorted figures and tension. Example: **El Greco** (in Spain).
- **Baroque** (from c. 1600): dramatic, emotional, grand, full of **movement and contrasts of light**; closely tied to the **Catholic Reformation** and absolute monarchs, to inspire awe and faith. Examples: **Caravaggio** (dramatic chiaroscuro), **Bernini** (St. Peter's colonnade, *Ecstasy of St. Teresa*), **Rubens** (Flemish), **Velázquez** (Spanish court).
- **Dutch Golden Age** painting for **middle-class Protestant** buyers: portraits, landscapes, and **everyday scenes**, by **Rembrandt** (*The Night Watch*, 1642) and **Vermeer**.
- The **witch hunts** peaked c. 1560–1660; tens of thousands of people, mostly **women**, were executed, a sign of religious anxiety and social stress.

## Big picture for the exam
Know **who explored where and when** (Dias 1488, Columbus 1492, da Gama 1498, Magellan 1519–22), **Tordesillas**, **conquistadors**, the **Columbian Exchange** and **slave trade**; the **Price Revolution**, **joint-stock companies**, **mercantilism**; **Philip II** (Lepanto, Dutch Revolt, Armada); **France** (St. Bartholomew's, Henry IV, **Edict of Nantes**, Richelieu); **Stuart England** (divine right, Petition of Right, Civil War); the **Thirty Years' War phases** and **Westphalia's terms**; and the early **Scientific Revolution** (Copernicus → Kepler → Galileo; Bacon vs. Descartes).

=== compare
| | Francis Bacon | René Descartes |
|---|---|---|
| Method | **Inductive**: from many observations to general laws | **Deductive**: from certain first principles to conclusions |
| Starting point | Experiment, experience (**empiricism**) | Doubt everything; "I think, therefore I am" (**rationalism**) |
| Key work | *Novum Organum* (1620) | *Discourse on Method* (1637) |

| | Old view (Ptolemy, Aristotle, Galen) | New view |
|---|---|---|
| Universe | Earth at center; perfect circles | Sun at center (Copernicus); ellipses (Kepler) |
| Heavens | Perfect, unchanging | Moon mountains, Jupiter's moons (Galileo) |
| Body | Galen's anatomy | Vesalius' dissections; Harvey's circulation |
| Source of truth | Ancient authority | Observation, math, experiment |
