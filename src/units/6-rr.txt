=== lesson
## 1. Why the Renaissance began in Italy
"Renaissance" means **rebirth**, meaning the rebirth of interest in the art and learning of **classical Greece and Rome**. It began in northern Italy around **1350** and spread north after about 1450.

>> Italian city-states grow rich from Mediterranean **trade and banking**; Roman ruins all around; Greek scholars arrive from Byzantium (especially after **1453**) | Wealthy merchants, princes, and popes become **patrons**, spending on scholars, artists, and buildings to win prestige | A burst of classical learning and art centered in **Florence**, then Rome and Venice

**Italian politics:** no unified kingdom. Five major powers: **Florence** (a republic dominated by the **Medici** banking family: Cosimo and **Lorenzo "the Magnificent"**), **Venice** (a merchant republic led by an elected doge), **Milan** (dukes, the Sforza), the **Papal States**, and the **Kingdom of Naples**. The **Peace of Lodi (1454)** created a **balance of power** among them, and Italians developed modern **diplomacy** with permanent ambassadors. The balance broke when **France invaded in 1494**, beginning decades of **Italian Wars**. In Florence, the fiery Dominican friar **Savonarola** briefly ruled (1494–1498), burned luxuries in the "bonfire of the vanities," and was himself burned. In **1527**, mutinous troops of Emperor **Charles V** **sacked Rome**.

## 2. Humanism
**Humanism** was an educational and intellectual movement: the study of the classics, the ***studia humanitatis*** (grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, moral philosophy), to produce eloquent, virtuous, active citizens. Humanists were **not anti-religious**, but they focused on **human potential and achievement in this world** (**secularism**) and celebrated **individualism**.

- **Petrarch** (1304–1374), the "**father of humanism**": hunted down lost classical manuscripts, wrote letters to the long-dead Cicero, wrote sonnets in Italian, and called the centuries after Rome a "dark" age.
- **Boccaccio:** the ***Decameron***, worldly tales told by people fleeing the Black Death.
- **Civic humanism** (Leonardo **Bruni**, Florence): classical learning should serve active public life in a republic.
- **Lorenzo Valla** used **textual analysis (philology)** to prove that the **Donation of Constantine**, a document supporting papal claims to rule the West, was a **forgery** (1440).
- **Marsilio Ficino** led the **Platonic Academy** in Florence and translated Plato.
- **Pico della Mirandola**, ***Oration on the Dignity of Man*** (1486): humans have free will to shape themselves, rising toward angels or sinking toward beasts.
- **Baldassare Castiglione**, ***The Book of the Courtier*** (1528): the ideal gentleman is skilled in arms, letters, music, and conversation, all done with effortless grace (*sprezzatura*): the "**Renaissance man**."
- **Niccolò Machiavelli**, ***The Prince*** (1513): a ruler must be guided by **political realism**, not Christian morality; "it is **safer to be feared than loved**"; a prince must sometimes do evil to preserve the state. Machiavelli is called the father of modern **political science**.

Educated elite women could gain humanist learning, and some were major patrons (**Isabella d'Este**), but most women's legal and social position did not improve.

## 3. Renaissance art
Renaissance art featured **realism**, **classical themes and forms**, **individual portraits**, and new techniques: **linear perspective** (a vanishing point creating depth), **chiaroscuro** (light and shadow), and **oil paint**.

| Artist | Known for |
|---|---|
| **Giotto** (c. 1300) | Forerunner: natural, emotional figures |
| **Brunelleschi** | Worked out **linear perspective**; built the great **dome of Florence Cathedral** |
| **Masaccio** | First painter to use perspective systematically (*Holy Trinity*) |
| **Donatello** | Bronze ***David***: first freestanding nude statue since antiquity |
| **Botticelli** | ***Birth of Venus***: classical mythology for Medici patrons |
| **Leonardo da Vinci** | ***Last Supper***, ***Mona Lisa***, notebooks of anatomy and inventions: the ultimate "Renaissance man" |
| **Michelangelo** | Marble ***David***, **Sistine Chapel ceiling** (for Pope Julius II), dome of St. Peter's |
| **Raphael** | ***School of Athens*** (Plato and Aristotle at the center); Madonnas |
| **Titian** | Venetian master of rich color |

The **High Renaissance** (c. 1490–1527) centered on **Rome**, where popes such as **Julius II** and **Leo X** (a Medici) commissioned masterpieces and rebuilt **St. Peter's Basilica**.

## 4. The Northern Renaissance and the printing press
>> **Johannes Gutenberg** of Mainz develops **movable metal type** (c. 1450); the **Gutenberg Bible** appears c. 1455 | Books become cheap and plentiful; by 1500 millions have been printed across Europe | **Literacy** spreads, scholarship becomes standardized, and new ideas (including Luther's) **spread faster than authorities can suppress them**

Northern humanists, the **Christian humanists**, used humanist scholarship to **reform Christianity** by returning to its original sources (*ad fontes*: the Bible and early Church Fathers in their original languages).

- **Desiderius Erasmus** (Dutch): ***In Praise of Folly*** (1509) satirized corrupt clergy, empty rituals, and superstition; published an accurate **Greek New Testament** (1516). He wanted **reform from within** and never left the Catholic Church. Contemporaries said "**Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched**."
- **Thomas More** (England): ***Utopia*** (1516), an imaginary island society with common property, criticizing European greed and injustice. He was **executed in 1535** for refusing to accept Henry VIII as head of the English Church.
- **François Rabelais** (France): bawdy satire in *Gargantua and Pantagruel*.

**Northern art** emphasized **detailed realism**, everyday life, and religious devotion, often in **oil paint** (perfected by Flemish painters):

- **Jan van Eyck:** oil-painting pioneer; ***Arnolfini Portrait*** (intense detail).
- **Albrecht Dürer** (Germany): brought Italian techniques north; famous **woodcuts and engravings**.
- **Hans Holbein the Younger:** portraits of **Henry VIII** and Thomas More.
- **Pieter Bruegel the Elder:** scenes of **peasant life** (*Peasant Wedding*).

## 5. The New Monarchies (c. 1450–1550)
After the chaos of the 1300s, kings in Western Europe built stronger, more centralized states. Common methods: **standing armies** (paid troops with cannon, which could knock down nobles' castles), **new taxes**, royal courts and **middle-class officials** loyal to the crown, curbing the great nobles, and **controlling the Church in their lands**.

- **France:** **Louis XI** ("the Spider King," 1461–1483) expanded royal territory and taxes after the Hundred Years' War. **Francis I**'s **Concordat of Bologna (1516)** let the king **appoint French bishops and abbots**, giving him control of the French Church without breaking with Rome.
- **England:** **Henry VII**, first **Tudor** king, won the **Battle of Bosworth (1485)**, ending the **Wars of the Roses**. He tamed the nobles (the **Court of Star Chamber** tried powerful men without juries), filled the treasury, and avoided costly wars.
- **Spain:** the marriage of **Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile (1469)** joined Spain's two largest kingdoms. In **1492** they conquered **Granada**, completing the **Reconquista**, **expelled the Jews** (Muslims were forced to convert or leave soon after), and sponsored **Columbus**. The **Spanish Inquisition (1478)**, controlled by the crown, enforced religious unity.
- **Holy Roman Empire:** the **Habsburgs** grew through **marriage alliances** rather than central government. Their heir **Charles V** inherited **Spain**, its American empire, Naples, the **Netherlands**, and **Austria**, and was elected emperor in **1519**: the most powerful ruler in Europe, but the German princes stayed largely independent.

## 6. The Protestant Reformation
**Long-term causes:**

- **Church abuses:** **simony**, **pluralism** (one man holding several church offices), **absenteeism** (not living in one's diocese), **nepotism**, and poorly educated priests.
- **Worldly Renaissance popes:** Alexander VI (Borgia), the warrior pope Julius II, and Leo X spent lavishly.
- **Indulgences:** remissions of punishment for sin, increasingly **sold** for cash.
- **Humanist criticism** and Bible study (Erasmus), the **printing press**, the earlier movements of **Wycliffe and Hus**.
- **Political:** princes and kings resented papal taxes and wanted control of Church lands and offices; growing **national feeling**, especially in Germany.

>> To fund the rebuilding of **St. Peter's**, the friar **Johann Tetzel** sells indulgences in Germany ("As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs") | **Martin Luther**, a monk and professor at **Wittenberg**, posts the **Ninety-Five Theses** (October 31, **1517**) challenging indulgences | Printed copies spread across Germany in weeks; a theological debate becomes a revolt against Rome

### Martin Luther (1483–1546)
Luther's key teachings:

- **Justification by faith alone (*sola fide*):** salvation comes only through faith, a free gift of God's grace, **not by good works** or buying indulgences.
- **Scripture alone (*sola scriptura*):** the **Bible** is the only authority, not popes or councils.
- **Priesthood of all believers:** all Christians have direct access to God; clergy are not a separate holy class and **may marry**. (Luther married a former nun, Katharina von Bora.)
- Only **two sacraments** (baptism and the Eucharist), with Christ **really present** in the bread and wine, though Luther rejected transubstantiation.

The confrontation: excommunicated in **1521**, he refused to recant at the **Diet of Worms (1521)** before Emperor **Charles V** ("Here I stand; I can do no other"). The **Edict of Worms** made him an outlaw, but **Frederick the Wise**, elector of **Saxony**, protected him in Wartburg Castle, where he **translated the New Testament into German**.

**German Peasants' War (1524–1525):** peasants cited Luther's ideas of Christian freedom to demand an end to serfdom. Luther **sided with the princes**, urging them to crush the revolt. From then on Lutheranism spread **with the support of princes**, who also gained control of church property.

The name "**Protestant**" comes from princes who **protested** an imperial decision at the Diet of Speyer (1529). After years of war between Charles V and the Protestant **Schmalkaldic League**, the **Peace of Augsburg (1555)** ruled that each German prince could choose **Lutheranism or Catholicism** for his territory: ***cuius regio, eius religio*** ("whose realm, his religion"). **Calvinism was not included**, a problem that helped cause the Thirty Years' War (Unit 7).

### Zwingli, Calvin, and the radicals
- **Ulrich Zwingli** (Zurich): even more radical in stripping churches of images; taught that the Eucharist is only a **symbolic** remembrance. He and Luther **disagreed at Marburg (1529)**, so Protestants never united. Zwingli died in battle (1531).
- **John Calvin** (a Frenchman in **Geneva**): ***Institutes of the Christian Religion*** (1536). Central doctrine: **predestination**, meaning God has already chosen the "**elect**" for salvation. Geneva became a model Protestant city governed by strict moral discipline (the **consistory** of pastors and elders policed behavior). Calvinism spread internationally: **Huguenots** (France), **Presbyterians** in Scotland (**John Knox**), **Puritans** (England), and the **Dutch Reformed** Church. Later writers (Max Weber) linked Calvinist discipline and hard work to the rise of capitalism.
- **Anabaptists** ("re-baptizers"): **adult baptism** only, **separation of church and state**, often **pacifism** and shared property. Persecuted by Catholics and Protestants alike, especially after radicals seized **Münster (1534–1535)**. Descendants include the **Mennonites** (and later Amish).

### The English Reformation: politics first
>> **Henry VIII** needs a male heir; his wife **Catherine of Aragon** has given him only a daughter (Mary) | The pope refuses an annulment, under pressure from Catherine's nephew, Emperor **Charles V** | Parliament's **Act of Supremacy (1534)** makes the **king head of the Church of England**; Henry marries **Anne Boleyn**

Henry **dissolved the monasteries** (1536–1540) and sold their lands to nobles and gentry, who then supported the break. Doctrine stayed largely **Catholic** under Henry. His son **Edward VI** pushed Protestantism (Archbishop **Cranmer**'s **Book of Common Prayer**). **Mary I** restored Catholicism and burned about 300 Protestants ("**Bloody Mary**"). **Elizabeth I** (1558–1603) set a moderate, Protestant **Elizabethan Settlement** (the Church of England as a "middle way," with the monarch as Supreme Governor).

## 7. The Catholic Reformation (Counter-Reformation)
The Catholic Church responded with genuine reform **and** firm defense of doctrine.

- **Pope Paul III** (1534–1549) appointed reformers and called the **Council of Trent (1545–1563)**:
  - **Reaffirmed** doctrine: salvation by **faith and good works**, **all seven sacraments**, **transubstantiation**, the authority of **both Scripture and Church tradition**, the Latin Vulgate, and **Latin Mass**.
  - **Reformed** abuses: ended the sale of indulgences (while keeping indulgences), required bishops to **live in their dioceses**, and created **seminaries** to train priests.
- **Jesuits (Society of Jesus):** founded by **Ignatius of Loyola**, a former Spanish soldier; approved **1540**. Organized like an army with absolute **obedience to the pope**, they excelled at **education** (schools and universities), **missionary work** (**Francis Xavier** in India and Japan), and winning back areas like Poland and southern Germany.
- **Roman Inquisition (1542)** prosecuted heresy; the ***Index of Prohibited Books*** (1559) banned dangerous works.
- New piety: **Teresa of Ávila** (Carmelite reform, mysticism); the **Ursulines** educated girls.
