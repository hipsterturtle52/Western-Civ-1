=== lesson
## 1. Three heirs of Rome
By 600 CE the old Roman world had split into three civilizations that the exam constantly compares:

- **Byzantine Empire:** the surviving Eastern Roman Empire, Greek-speaking, Orthodox Christian, capital **Constantinople**.
- **Islamic world:** a new faith from Arabia that built an empire from Spain to India within a century.
- **Latin West:** Germanic kingdoms, Latin-speaking clergy, the pope in Rome, and a poorer, rural economy.

## 2. The Byzantine Empire
**Constantinople** controlled the straits between Europe and Asia and the trade routes linking the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. It was rich, well defended by massive land walls, and run by a trained bureaucracy. The **gold coin** (the solidus or "bezant") was trusted across the Mediterranean.

**Justinian** (ruled 527–565), with his influential wife **Theodora**, is the emperor to know:

- **Corpus Juris Civilis** ("Body of Civil Law," **Justinian's Code**): a commission led by Tribonian collected and organized centuries of Roman law. It became the basis of law in most of continental Europe.
- **Hagia Sophia** ("Holy Wisdom," 537): an enormous domed church.
- **Nika Riots (532):** rioters nearly toppled him; Theodora convinced him to stay and fight, and the army crushed the revolt.
- **Reconquest:** his general **Belisarius** retook **North Africa** from the Vandals and **Italy** from the Ostrogoths. But the wars and the **Plague of Justinian (541)** drained the empire, and most of Italy fell to the **Lombards** soon after (568).

**Byzantine features to remember:**

- **Caesaropapism:** the emperor controlled both state **and** church, appointing the **patriarch** of Constantinople. (In the West, popes and kings were separate and often rivals.)
- **Greek fire:** a secret liquid incendiary weapon that destroyed enemy fleets, helping save the city in the Arab sieges (674–678, 717–718).
- **Iconoclasm (726–843):** Emperor Leo III banned religious images (**icons**) as idolatry. The fight split Byzantine society and angered the pope; icons were restored in 843.
- **Missions to the Slavs:** the brothers **Cyril and Methodius** created a Slavic alphabet (the ancestor of **Cyrillic**). Prince **Vladimir of Kiev** converted to Orthodox Christianity in **988**, tying Russia to Byzantine religion and culture.

>> Centuries of disagreement over **papal authority**, the *filioque* clause in the creed, clergy marriage, leavened bread, and language (Latin vs. Greek) | In **1054** the pope's envoy and the patriarch **excommunicate** each other: the **Great Schism** | Christianity splits into the **Roman Catholic** West and the **Eastern Orthodox** East

**Legacy:** for 1,000 years after 476 Byzantium **shielded Europe** from eastern invaders and **preserved Greek classics and Roman law**. It fell to the **Ottoman Turks in 1453**.

## 3. The rise of Islam
**Muhammad** (c. 570–632) was a merchant of **Mecca**, a trading city in Arabia with a shrine, the **Kaaba**. Around **610** he began receiving revelations he believed came from God (**Allah**) through the angel Gabriel. Mecca's leaders persecuted his followers, so in **622** he moved to **Medina**. This journey, the **Hijra**, marks **year 1 of the Muslim calendar**. By 630 he had returned to take Mecca, and by his death in 632 most of Arabia had accepted Islam ("submission" to God).

**Beliefs:** strict **monotheism**; Muhammad as the **last prophet** in a line including Abraham, Moses, and Jesus; the **Quran** as God's exact words. Jews and Christians were "**People of the Book**."

**Five Pillars of Islam:**

1. **Shahada:** declaring faith ("There is no god but God, and Muhammad is his messenger").
2. **Salat:** prayer five times a day, facing Mecca.
3. **Zakat:** giving alms to the poor.
4. **Sawm:** fasting during the month of **Ramadan**.
5. **Hajj:** pilgrimage to Mecca once in a lifetime if able.

### Expansion and the caliphates
After Muhammad, leaders called **caliphs** ("successors") ruled. Arab armies swept out of Arabia and took **Syria, Egypt, and Persia** within about 20 years.

>> Byzantine and Persian (Sassanid) empires exhausted by decades of war against each other | Arab Muslim armies, united by faith, conquer swiftly; they **tax rather than force conversion** of Christians and Jews (who pay the *jizya* tax as protected *dhimmi*) | Many conquered peoples accept Muslim rule, and many gradually convert

**Sunni–Shia split:** a dispute over **who should succeed Muhammad**. **Shia** Muslims believe leadership belonged to **Ali** (Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law) and his descendants; **Sunni** Muslims (the large majority) accepted caliphs chosen by the community. Ali's son **Husayn** was killed at **Karbala (680)**, hardening the split.

| Caliphate | Capital | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Umayyad** (661–750) | **Damascus** | Conquered North Africa and **Spain (711)**; stopped in France at **Tours (732)** and at Constantinople (717–718) |
| **Abbasid** (750–1258) | **Baghdad** (founded 762) | **Islamic Golden Age**; **House of Wisdom**; Baghdad sacked by the **Mongols in 1258** |
| Umayyad Spain (al-Andalus) | **Córdoba** | Survivor of the Umayyad family; brilliant culture where Muslims, Jews, and Christians interacted |

### The Islamic Golden Age
Scholars in Baghdad's **House of Wisdom** translated **Greek** works (Aristotle, Galen, Euclid, Ptolemy) into Arabic, preserving and extending them. Many later reached Europe through **Spain and Sicily**.

- **Al-Khwarizmi:** **algebra** (from Arabic *al-jabr*); our word "algorithm" comes from his name.
- **Number system:** Muslims adopted **Indian numerals including zero**, which Europeans called "Arabic numerals."
- **Ibn Sina (Avicenna):** the ***Canon of Medicine***, a standard textbook in European universities for centuries.
- **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** of Córdoba (12th century): famous commentaries on **Aristotle** that influenced Christian scholastics.
- Advances in astronomy (astrolabe), optics, chemistry, and huge trade networks.

## 4. The early medieval West
In the West, cities shrank, roads decayed, trade slowed, and literacy collapsed outside the Church. The **Church** became the main link to Roman civilization.

**Monasticism** was crucial. **Benedict of Nursia** wrote the **Benedictine Rule** (c. 529, Monte Cassino): monks took vows of **poverty, chastity, and obedience** and followed a balanced routine of prayer, work, and study ("**pray and work**"). Monasteries **copied manuscripts**, ran schools and hospitals, cleared land, and sent missionaries. **Irish monks** (Ireland was converted by **St. Patrick** in the 400s) kept learning alive and produced illuminated manuscripts like the **Book of Kells**. The English monk **Bede** wrote a famous ***Ecclesiastical History of the English People*** (731).

**Pope Gregory I, "the Great"** (590–604) strengthened the papacy: he governed the city of Rome, organized Church lands, and sent missionaries to convert the **Anglo-Saxons** in England (597).

## 5. The Franks and Charlemagne
The **Franks** in Gaul became the most successful Germanic kingdom.

>> **Clovis** (ruled 481–511) unites the Franks and converts directly to **Catholic** (not Arian) Christianity, c. 496 | He wins the support of the pope, bishops, and his Gallo-Roman subjects | The Franks become the Church's chief protectors in the West

Clovis' **Merovingian** descendants grew weak ("do-nothing kings"). Real power passed to the **mayors of the palace**:

- **Charles Martel** ("the Hammer") defeated a Muslim army at **Tours (Poitiers), 732**, halting Muslim expansion into France.
- His son **Pepin the Short** deposed the last Merovingian with the **pope's approval (751)** and was anointed king, founding the **Carolingian** dynasty. In return he defeated the Lombards and gave the pope land in central Italy (the **Donation of Pepin, 756**), the origin of the **Papal States**.

**Charlemagne** ("Charles the Great," ruled 768–814) built the largest Western state since Rome:

- Conquered the **Saxons** (forcing conversion), the **Lombards** in Italy, and Bavaria, and fought Muslims in Spain (the *Song of Roland* romanticizes a defeat there in 778).
- Governed through local **counts**, checked by royal inspectors, the ***missi dominici*** ("envoys of the lord"), traveling in pairs (a noble and a bishop). Capital: **Aachen**.
- On **Christmas Day, 800**, **Pope Leo III** crowned him **"Emperor of the Romans."** This revived the idea of a Western empire, suggested that **popes made emperors** (a claim later popes would use), and offended the Byzantines.
- **Carolingian Renaissance:** a palace school led by the English scholar **Alcuin of York**; monks copied classical and Christian texts in a clear new script, **Carolingian minuscule** (the ancestor of our lowercase letters). Charlemagne himself struggled to write (we know this from his biographer **Einhard**).

>> Frankish custom divides land among all sons | **Treaty of Verdun (843)** splits the empire among Charlemagne's three grandsons | West Francia becomes the core of **France**, East Francia of **Germany**, and the middle kingdom (Lotharingia, Italy) becomes a battleground

## 6. New invasions (c. 800–1000)
Europe was attacked from three sides:

- **Vikings** (Norsemen) from Scandinavia, in fast, shallow **longships**: raided monasteries (Lindisfarne, **793**), settled **Normandy** (granted to Rollo in **911**), the English "Danelaw," Iceland, and Greenland, briefly reached **North America (Vinland)**, and helped found the state of **Kievan Rus'**. In England, **Alfred the Great** of Wessex (871–899) resisted them.
- **Magyars** (Hungarians) on horseback from the east, defeated at **Lechfeld (955)** by the German king **Otto I**, who was crowned emperor in **962**, the start of the **Holy Roman Empire**.
- **Muslims (Saracens)** raiding from North Africa, Sicily, and the Mediterranean.

## 7. Feudalism and manorialism
With kings too weak to defend everyone, protection became **local**.

>> Carolingian collapse + Viking, Magyar, and Muslim raids | Local lords with castles and mounted **knights** provide defense in exchange for loyalty and service | **Feudalism**: power is decentralized among nobles bound by personal oaths

**Feudalism** (political/military system among **nobles**):

- A **lord** grants a **fief** (usually land) to a **vassal**.
- In a ceremony of **homage and fealty**, the vassal swears loyalty and owes **military service** (often ~40 days a year), **counsel** at the lord's court, and **aids** (payments, e.g., to ransom the lord or knight his son).
- The lord owes the vassal **protection** and justice.
- Vassals could have their own vassals (**subinfeudation**), and a man could be vassal to several lords, which made loyalties messy.
- Roots: the Germanic ***comitatus*** (warrior loyalty) + late Roman land-for-service arrangements.

**Manorialism** (economic system):

- The **manor** was a largely **self-sufficient** farming estate: the lord's land (**demesne**), peasants' strips in **open fields**, a mill, a church, and common woods and pasture.
- **Serfs** were **bound to the land**: not slaves (they couldn't be sold apart from the land and had customary rights to their plots), but not free to leave. They owed **labor** on the demesne (often 3 days a week), a share of crops, and fees (**banalities**) for using the lord's mill, oven, and winepress, plus the **tithe** (one-tenth) to the Church.
- **Technology** gradually raised output: the **heavy plow** for wet northern soils, the **horse collar**, and the **three-field system** (one field winter grain, one spring crops like beans and oats, one fallow), which increased yields and improved diets.

| | Feudalism | Manorialism |
|---|---|---|
| What | Political & military relationships | Economic system |
| Between | Lords and vassals (all nobles) | Lords and serfs (peasants) |
| Exchange | Fief ↔ military service, loyalty | Protection and land use ↔ labor, crops, fees |
| Bond | Personal oath (homage & fealty) | Hereditary obligation to the land |
